TOPIC:
Founders confuse loyalty with obligation regarding early employees who've stopped performing

HOOK:
Loyalty to an Early Employee Isn't the Same as an Obligation to Keep Them Forever

INTRO:
Founders feel a debt to the people who joined first. Point 2 is where that debt gets misapplied.

POINT 1:
Gratitude for someone's early belief in the company is real and deserved, and it's a different thing from a lifetime performance exemption.

POINT 2:
Keeping an underperforming early employee out of loyalty quietly costs the rest of the team, who notice the double standard.

POINT 3:
The founders who honor early loyalty well do it through genuine gratitude and fair transitions, not by avoiding a hard, overdue conversation.

CAPTION:
The people who joined first, who believed in the idea before there was much evidence to believe in, earn real gratitude. That gratitude is genuine and deserved - and it's a different thing entirely from an unspoken obligation to keep them in the role forever, regardless of performance.

Holding onto an underperforming early employee out of loyalty has a cost that's easy to underweight: the rest of the team notices the double standard, and it quietly erodes the fairness the culture depends on.

The founders who honor early loyalty well express it through genuine appreciation and a respectful, honest transition when it's needed - not by avoiding a conversation that's overdue.
