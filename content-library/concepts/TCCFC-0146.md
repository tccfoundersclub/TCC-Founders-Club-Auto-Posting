TOPIC:
Founders never practicing saying "I was wrong" costs them credibility over time

HOOK:
When's the Last Time You Said "I Was Wrong" Out Loud to Your Team?

INTRO:
Admitting a mistake feels like it costs authority. Point 2 is why the opposite is usually true.

POINT 1:
Avoiding "I was wrong" in favor of reframing or quietly moving on protects short-term authority and costs long-term trust.

POINT 2:
A team that never hears a founder admit a mistake starts to doubt whether the founder can actually see their own errors at all.

POINT 3:
The founders who build the most durable authority say "I was wrong" clearly and specifically, without over-apologizing or under-owning it.

CAPTION:
Admitting "I was wrong" out loud to the team feels like it costs authority - safer to quietly pivot, reframe, or just move on without naming it directly. That instinct protects authority in the short term and costs something bigger over time.

A team that never once hears their founder own a real mistake starts to wonder, quietly, whether that founder can actually see their own errors at all - or whether every setback gets explained away instead of owned.

The founders who build the most durable, lasting authority say "I was wrong" clearly and specifically when it's true, without over-apologizing and without underselling it either.
